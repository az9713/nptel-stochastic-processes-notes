01 · Counting fluctuations: discrete distributions and cumulantsReviewed

Read chapter 1 · Reviewed

Lectures 1, 2 · Prerequisites: entry prerequisites

Distinguish independent particle counts from thermal bunching using equal mean counts.

Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson, Skellam, negative binomial; generating functions, moments, and cumulants.

Core result 1: Independent trials and the rare-event limit

For n independent Bernoulli trials with success probability p, the count has probability-generating function (1-p+p*z)^n; its mean is n*p and variance n*p*(1-p). With n*p fixed at lambda as n tends to infinity, its law converges to Poisson(lambda).

Boundary. Independence and rare-event scaling are hypotheses; an arbitrary event count is not Poisson.

Core result 2: Bunching, summation, and signed counts

A geometric count on nonnegative integers with mean mu has variance mu*(1+mu). A sum of r independent such counts is negative binomial for positive integer r. The difference of independent Poisson counts of means lambda_plus and lambda_minus has mean lambda_plus-lambda_minus and variance lambda_plus+lambda_minus.

Boundary. State support and parameter conventions; the integer-sum construction alone does not establish every real-shape parameterization.

Core result 3: Cumulants expose independence

For a random variable whose moment-generating function exists near zero, cumulants are derivatives at zero of its logarithm. Independent sums add cumulants. Translation changes only the first cumulant; cumulants of order k scale by the kth power under scalar multiplication.

Boundary. A moment-generating function need not exist; later chapters use characteristic functions for heavy tails.

Laboratory. Compare exact probability masses and cumulants with seeded sampling; diagnose when equal means conceal different variances.

02 · Densities, transformations, and characteristic functionsReviewed

Read chapter 2 · Reviewed

Lectures 3 · Prerequisites: 1

Convert a Gaussian velocity model into the kinetic-energy distribution of an ideal gas.

Continuous densities, characteristic functions, Gaussian moments and cumulants, ideal-gas energy.

Core result 1: Probability transforms with every preimage

For a differentiable transformation y=g(x), a continuous input density transforms to the sum of input density divided by absolute derivative over regular inverse branches. The branch formula applies away from critical values, with singular behavior treated by integration.

Boundary. A noninvertible transformation requires all preimages; atoms and flat branches need separate treatment.

Core result 2: Characteristic functions and the Gaussian law

The characteristic function of a real random variable is the expectation of exp(i*k*X) for real k and always exists. A Gaussian with mean mu and variance sigma squared has characteristic function exp(i*k*mu-sigma squared*k squared/2). Its cumulants beyond order two vanish.

Boundary. Differentiation under expectation needs moment conditions; existence of the characteristic function does not imply existence of moments.

Core result 3: Energy as a sum of squared Gaussian coordinates

If d independent velocity components are centered Gaussians of variance k_B*T/m, the kinetic energy m times squared speed divided by two has a gamma density of shape d/2 and scale k_B*T. Its mean is d*k_B*T/2.

Boundary. This is classical, nonrelativistic equilibrium with independent quadratic degrees of freedom.

Laboratory. Compare change-of-variable formulas with sampled velocities and energy histograms, including Jacobian normalization.

03 · The central limit theorem and the diffusion scaleReviewed

Read chapter 3 · Reviewed

Lectures 4 · Prerequisites: 1, 2

Decide when a many-step random flight can be replaced by a Gaussian propagator.

Gaussian sums, finite-variance central limit theorem, uniform sums, random flights, diffusion scaling.

Core result 1: Convolution becomes multiplication

For independent real variables, the characteristic function of their sum is the product of their characteristic functions. Independent Gaussian sums are Gaussian with summed means and variances.

Boundary. Dependence invalidates factorization even when the marginals are Gaussian.

Core result 2: A finite-variance central limit theorem

For independent identically distributed variables with finite mean mu and positive finite variance sigma squared, the centered sum divided by sigma times the square root of the number of terms converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian.

Boundary. Convergence in distribution gives neither a universal finite-sample rate nor uniformly correct rare-event tails.

Core result 3: The Brownian scaling of a lattice walk

For independent unbiased steps of length ell taken every delta_t, the variance after elapsed time t is ell squared*t/delta_t on grid times. Holding ell squared/(2*delta_t)=D fixed yields the one-dimensional diffusion kernel in the continuum scaling limit.

Boundary. A continuum approximation loses finite-speed support and needs a stated mode of convergence.

Laboratory. Measure distributional convergence and lattice-to-diffusion error across step sizes; compare the central region with tails.

04 · Stable laws, heavy tails, and infinite divisibilityReviewed

Read chapter 4 · Reviewed

Lectures 5 · Prerequisites: 1, 2, 3

Determine whether averaging repeated measurements actually reduces their scatter.

Stable-law definitions, Gaussian/Cauchy/Levy examples, divisibility and infinite divisibility.

Core result 1: Stable scaling

A symmetric strictly stable law with index alpha in (0,2] and characteristic function exp(-c*absolute(k)^alpha), c positive, is closed under independent sums: n equal copies rescale by n raised to 1/alpha without centering.

Boundary. Use this symmetric subclass for the proof; general skewed stable laws need explicit centering and parameter conventions.

Core result 2: Why the sample mean may not concentrate

The mean of n independent centered Cauchy variables with scale s has the same Cauchy law and scale s for every positive integer n. For a centered Gaussian of variance sigma squared, the sample mean has variance sigma squared/n.

Boundary. The Cauchy mean and variance do not exist; a sample average is still a defined random variable.

Core result 3: Divisibility is a property of the whole law

A law is infinitely divisible when, for each positive integer n, it is the law of a sum of n independent identically distributed variables. Poisson laws have this property by dividing their intensity; a nondegenerate fixed-trial binomial law is not infinitely divisible on the real line.

Boundary. Being decomposable into a particular number of summands is weaker than infinite divisibility; the one-sided Levy example is separate from the symmetric subclass.

Laboratory. Compare Gaussian and Cauchy sample means using quantiles rather than nonexistent Cauchy moments.

05 · From random variables to Markov state descriptionsReviewed

Read chapter 5 · Reviewed

Lectures 6 · Prerequisites: 1, 2

Explain why matching single-time histograms does not determine the evolution of a system.

Joint and conditional laws, stationarity, Markov property, chains, Chapman-Kolmogorov, equilibrium relaxation.

Core result 1: A process is a family of joint laws

Strict stationarity means invariance of every finite-dimensional joint distribution under a common time shift. Constancy of the one-time marginal alone does not imply strict stationarity.

Boundary. Wide-sense stationarity is a different, second-moment condition; Gaussianity is needed to recover the full law from first and second moments.

Core result 2: State sufficiency and composition

For a time-homogeneous Markov chain with K_ij equal to the probability of the next state being i given current state j, distribution columns evolve by multiplication by K. Multi-step transitions satisfy K raised to (n+m)=K raised to n times K raised to m.

Boundary. An observed coordinate of a Markov process need not itself be Markov.

Core result 3: Equilibrium is not automatic convergence

A stationary probability column pi satisfies K*pi=pi. A finite irreducible aperiodic chain converges to its unique stationary distribution from every initial distribution; irreducibility without aperiodicity is insufficient.

Boundary. Infinite state spaces require additional recurrence conditions; a stationary vector alone does not prove mixing.

Laboratory. Construct processes with equal marginals and different correlations; evolve a finite transition matrix.

06 · Generators, detailed balance, and exactly soluble jump processesReviewed

Read chapter 6 · Reviewed

Lectures 7, 8 · Prerequisites: 1, 5

Predict relaxation, event counts, and displacement from transition rates rather than time-step probabilities.

Master-equation semigroups, stationary laws, detailed balance, dichotomous correlations, Poisson process, biased lattice walks.

Core result 1: A conservative generator

For a finite-state continuous-time chain with nonnegative off-diagonal rates Q_ij from j to i and zero column sums, the probability column satisfies dp/dt=Q*p and evolves by exp(t*Q). A stationary law satisfies Q*pi=0; detailed balance requires Q_ij*pi_j=Q_ji*pi_i.

Boundary. Detailed balance is sufficient for stationarity and stronger than it; infinite generators need nonexplosion and domain control.

Core result 2: Two states retain a correlation time

For a symmetric process taking values plus or minus a and switching at rate nu from either state, the stationary mean is zero and autocovariance is a squared times exp(-2*nu*absolute(tau)).

Boundary. Asymmetric switching changes the mean and variance; starting out of equilibrium changes two-time statistics.

Core result 3: Counting and displacement generating functions

A homogeneous Poisson process of rate lambda has count generating function exp(lambda*t*(z-1)). The difference of two independent Poisson processes with rates r_plus and r_minus has mean displacement ell*(r_plus-r_minus)*t and variance ell squared*(r_plus+r_minus)*t.

Boundary. Independent stationary increments and exponential waiting times distinguish these models from general renewal processes.

Laboratory. Compare exact matrix exponentials and generating functions with event-driven jump simulations.

07 · Lattice transport and state-dependent birth and deathReviewed

Read chapter 7 · Reviewed

Lectures 9 · Prerequisites: 1, 6

Identify which observables remain tractable when event rates depend on the present population.

Walk cumulants, higher-dimensional walks, one-step birth/death generators, nonstationary Poisson counts, linear rates.

Core result 1: Transport cumulants

For lattice displacement ell times the difference of independent Poisson counts with rates r_plus and r_minus, the kth displacement cumulant is ell raised to k times (r_plus+(-1) raised to k*r_minus)*t. Independent coordinate walks factorize their joint characteristic function.

Boundary. Coordinate independence is an assumption, not a consequence of living on a lattice.

Core result 2: One-step population generators

For births at rate lambda_n and deaths at rate delta_n in population state n, with delta_0=0, the probability equation is dp_n/dt=lambda_(n-1)*p_(n-1)+delta_(n+1)*p_(n+1)-(lambda_n+delta_n)*p_n, with nonexistent negative-state terms omitted.

Boundary. Unbounded state spaces can permit explosion; a formal conservative expression is insufficient without existence conditions.

Core result 3: Time-dependent intensity

A nonhomogeneous Poisson process with deterministic locally integrable rate lambda(t) has count mean and variance equal to the integrated rate from zero to t, and Poisson increments on disjoint intervals are independent.

Boundary. A random intensity defines a different model; replacing it by its expectation generally loses overdispersion.

Laboratory. Compare exact walk cumulants and moment equations with event-driven population simulations.

08 · Stationary populations, fluctuations, and physical balanceReviewed

Read chapter 8 · Reviewed

Lectures 10 · Prerequisites: 6, 7

Decide whether a rate model admits an equilibrium population or only a formal stationary sequence.

Birth/death stationary products; sedimentation, chemistry, radiation, population models; linear-rate moments.

Core result 1: The stationary product formula

For an irreducible birth-death chain on nonnegative integers with positive neighbor rates and zero boundary current, a stationary probability is proportional to the product from j=0 to n-1 of lambda_j/delta_(j+1), provided the sum of these weights is finite and the process is nonexplosive.

Boundary. A positive recurrence criterion cannot be replaced by writing down unnormalized weights.

Core result 2: Balance laws in physical examples

If nearest-neighbor rate ratios equal exp(-(E_(n+1)-E_n)/(k_B*T)), the zero-current stationary weights are proportional to exp(-E_n/(k_B*T)) whenever normalizable.

Boundary. Thermal balance requires the stated bath assumptions; population models need not be thermal or reversible.

Core result 3: Closed moment equations

For a nonexplosive birth-death process with finite required moments, the derivative of the mean equals the expectation of lambda_n-delta_n; the derivative of the second moment equals the expectation of (2*n+1)*lambda_n+(-2*n+1)*delta_n. Affine rates close these equations.

Boundary. Nonlinear rates generally introduce higher moments; closure is then an approximation requiring justification.

Laboratory. Reproduce immigration-death equilibrium and diagnose a nonnormalizable stationary candidate.

09 · From jump generators to diffusion equationsReviewed

Read chapter 9 · Reviewed

Lectures 11 · Prerequisites: 3, 6, 7, 8

Choose a continuum approximation that preserves drift, fluctuations, and probability current.

Continuous-state master equations, Kramers-Moyal, Pawula scope, Fokker-Planck, Smoluchowski, Wiener and SDE correspondence.

Core result 1: Conditional increment moments

For an Itô diffusion dX=a(X,t)*dt+b(X,t)*dW with scalar Wiener process W, sufficient coefficient regularity, and an existing smooth density rho, the forward equation is partial_t rho=-partial_x(a*rho)+(1/2)*partial_xx(b squared*rho).

Boundary. Noise amplitude b and diffusion coefficient b squared/2 differ; singular boundaries require boundary classification.

Core result 2: The controlled lattice limit

For a nearest-neighbor walk with spacing ell and rates r_plus and r_minus, drift is ell*(r_plus-r_minus) and diffusion coefficient is ell squared*(r_plus+r_minus)/2. A diffusive limit holds when these combinations approach finite values and higher jump effects vanish in the limit.

Boundary. Truncating the Kramers-Moyal series is not automatically a positive probability evolution; cite Pawula's precise theorem after verifying its source.

Core result 3: Current, confinement, and stationary density

For constant D positive and drift -M*U'(x) with mobility M positive, zero stationary current yields density proportional to exp(-M*U(x)/D), if normalizable. At thermal equilibrium D=M*k_B*T gives the Gibbs density.

Boundary. Zero-current equilibrium depends on the domain and boundary conditions; it is distinct from a driven steady state.

Laboratory. Match lattice drift and diffusion coefficients and measure continuum approximation error under grid refinement.

10 · Velocity relaxation and the emergence of spatial diffusionReviewed

Read chapter 10 · Reviewed

Lectures 12, 13 · Prerequisites: 2, 6, 9

Find when a Brownian particle's displacement changes from ballistic to diffusive.

Langevin/FPE correspondence, thermal noise strength, OU transition law, velocity correlation, MSD, Green-Kubo, joint density and Gaussian-Markov qualification.

Core result 1: The velocity transition law

For dV=-gamma*V*dt+sqrt(2*gamma*k_B*T/m)*dW, gamma positive, deterministic initial velocity v0, and independent Wiener increments, V(t) is Gaussian with mean v0*exp(-gamma*t) and variance (k_B*T/m)*(1-exp(-2*gamma*t)).

Boundary. gamma is a velocity relaxation rate, not the dimensional drag coefficient m*gamma.

Core result 2: Correlation and integrated displacement

With a thermal stationary initial velocity, the velocity autocovariance is (k_B*T/m)*exp(-gamma*absolute(tau)). The displacement variance is 2*D*(t-(1-exp(-gamma*t))/gamma), where D=k_B*T/(m*gamma).

Boundary. The quoted displacement law uses stationary initial velocity; a fixed initial velocity gives a different transient variance.

Core result 3: Green-Kubo and joint phase-space dynamics

For a stationary zero-mean velocity with integrable autocovariance C_v, displacement variance equals twice the integral from zero to t of (t-s)*C_v(s) ds, and its long-time diffusion coefficient equals the integral of C_v from zero to infinity. Position and velocity together form a Markov state in the inertial Langevin model.

Boundary. The position coordinate alone generally has memory at finite inertia; Doob classification requires a separate precise cited statement.

Laboratory. Compare exact OU updates with Euler discretization and recover the ballistic-to-diffusive displacement crossover.

11 · Confinement, phase space, and the overdamped limitReviewed

Read chapter 11 · Reviewed

Lectures 14 · Prerequisites: 9, 10

Determine whether eliminating velocity preserves the relaxation times of a trapped particle.

Coupled position/velocity Langevin equations, harmonic stationary density and rates, high friction, arbitrary-potential Kramers equation.

Core result 1: A linear stochastic oscillator

For dX=V*dt and dV=(-gamma*V-omega0 squared*X)*dt+sqrt(2*gamma*k_B*T/m)*dW, positive gamma and omega0 give stable mean dynamics whose eigenvalues solve r squared+gamma*r+omega0 squared=0.

Boundary. Stability of mean dynamics alone is not a proof of the full stationary distribution.

Core result 2: Kramers equilibrium

For a confining differentiable potential U, the inertial Langevin forward operator with force -U' and thermal noise admits a stationary phase-space density proportional to exp(-(m*v squared/2+U(x))/(k_B*T)), when the normalization is finite and boundary flux conditions hold.

Boundary. Stationary phase-space streaming need not vanish term by term; do not impose overdamped detailed-balance language on each current component.

Core result 3: Eliminating the fast velocity

For the harmonic model with gamma much larger than omega0, one relaxation rate approaches -gamma and the slow rate approaches -omega0 squared/gamma. On slow time scales, constant-friction thermal reduction gives dX=-U'(X)/(m*gamma)*dt+sqrt(2*k_B*T/(m*gamma))*dW.

Boundary. The reduction fails at momentum time scales; position-dependent friction needs additional care.

Laboratory. Compare inertial and overdamped harmonic correlations as damping changes; check equilibrium covariances.

12 · Wiener paths, roughness, and exceptional time statisticsReviewed

Read chapter 12 · Reviewed

Lectures 15 · Prerequisites: 3, 9, 10, 11

Understand why refining a Brownian trajectory makes its apparent velocity more erratic.

Vector Langevin dynamics, free and magnetic velocity correlations as a bridge; Wiener covariance, rough paths, Holder regularity, arcsine law, iterated logarithm, OU-Wiener relation.

Core result 1: Covariance without stationarity

A standard Wiener process starts at zero, has continuous paths and independent Gaussian increments of variance equal to elapsed time. Its covariance at nonnegative times s and t is min(s,t); the process is nonstationary although its increments are stationary.

Boundary. Continuous paths do not imply differentiable paths; Brownian motion is not finite-correlation colored noise.

Core result 2: Quadratic variation and path regularity

On uniform partitions of a fixed time interval, the sum of squared Wiener increments converges in mean square to the interval length. Finite-difference velocity over a step h has variance 1/h. Almost-sure differentiability and Holder claims require their own theorem statements and verified citations.

Boundary. A numerical path cannot prove almost-sure nondifferentiability, the arcsine law, or the iterated-logarithm theorem.

Core result 3: Gaussian time changes and exceptional observables

The zero-initial OU process solving dX=-gamma*X*dt+b*dW has the same finite-dimensional law as b*exp(-gamma*t)*B((exp(2*gamma*t)-1)/(2*gamma)), where B is a standard Wiener process. The equality is in law unless a coupling is constructed.

Boundary. Finite-dimensional law equivalence should not be confused with equality of arbitrary sampled paths.

Laboratory. Measure mesh-dependent finite-difference velocities and quadratic variation; illustrate occupation-time distributions without claiming proof.

13 · Itô transformations, radial diffusion, and multiplicative noiseReviewed

Read chapter 13 · Reviewed

Lectures 16 · Prerequisites: 2, 9, 12

Transform a noisy coordinate without silently losing its noise-induced drift.

Itô/FPE transformation, radial diffusion, Bessel and squared-Bessel conventions, powers/exponentials of Wiener motion, geometric Brownian motion, Black-Scholes boundary.

Core result 1: The second derivative survives

For scalar dX=a*dt+b*dW and a sufficiently smooth function g(x,t), Itô's rule gives dg=(partial_t g+a*partial_x g+(b squared/2)*partial_xx g)*dt+b*partial_x g*dW, under standard local integrability and existence hypotheses.

Boundary. This is the Itô convention; Stratonovich notation carries a different chain rule and drift conversion.

Core result 2: Radius and squared radius

For d-dimensional Brownian diffusion with coordinate noise amplitude sqrt(2*D), the radius away from zero has drift D*(d-1)/R and noise amplitude sqrt(2*D). Its square Z obeys dZ=2*d*D*dt+sqrt(8*D*Z)*dB.

Boundary. The radius formula is singular at zero; d=1 requires treatment of the reflecting local-time term. State which scaled process is called Bessel or squared Bessel.

Core result 3: Exponential transformations

For dS=a_S*S*dt+b_S*S*dW with positive initial S, the solution is S0*exp((a_S-b_S squared/2)*t+b_S*W(t)). Its expectation is S0*exp(a_S*t) when the coefficients are constant.

Boundary. The Black-Scholes equation additionally requires a market and pricing argument; it does not follow from the change of variables alone.

Laboratory. Contrast correct Itô transformed moments with an ordinary-chain-rule implementation; check positive geometric paths.

14 · Threshold crossings and extremes of smooth random signalsReviewed

Read chapter 14 · Reviewed

Lectures 17 · Prerequisites: 2, 5, 10, 11, 12

Estimate false alarm crossings from a fluctuating signal without counting every sampled sign change as a physical event.

Crossing counts and rates, up/down crossings, Brownian oscillator, extrema, introductory spectral examples.

Core result 1: Crossings require a derivative law

For a stationary differentiable real process with a regular joint density of value and derivative and finite crossing intensity, the mean upcrossing rate at level u is the integral over positive v of v times that joint density at (u,v).

Boundary. These regularity hypotheses exclude direct application to nondifferentiable Wiener and OU coordinates.

Core result 2: The Gaussian Rice rate

For a stationary zero-mean differentiable Gaussian process with positive value and derivative standard deviations sigma_X and sigma_V, the upcrossing rate is sigma_V/(2*pi*sigma_X) times exp(-u squared/(2*sigma_X squared)); stationarity makes value and derivative uncorrelated and hence independent.

Boundary. Finite derivative variance is essential; high-frequency smoothing changes crossing statistics.

Core result 3: Extrema and finite-window events

For a twice differentiable regular process, counting maxima can be expressed as zero crossings of the derivative weighted by negative second derivative. The expected number of level crossings does not by itself specify the probability of at least one crossing in a time window.

Boundary. Poisson approximations for rare crossings need additional dependence assumptions. The Brownian oscillator's velocity is not differentiable; do not apply this twice-differentiable extrema formula to it without smoothing.

Laboratory. Compare sampled crossing rates of an integrated velocity oscillator with the Gaussian Rice prediction and test sampling bias.

15 · Charged Brownian motion and anisotropic diffusionReviewed

Read chapter 15 · Reviewed

Lectures 18 · Prerequisites: 10, 11, 12

Explain how a magnetic field suppresses transverse diffusion without changing thermal speed variance.

Vector Langevin and FPE, rotating frame, velocity density and correlation matrix, Green-Kubo tensor, longitudinal and transverse diffusion.

Core result 1: Rotation and damping

For a uniform magnetic field, isotropic drag rate gamma positive, and thermal additive noise, the velocity drift matrix is -gamma times identity plus the antisymmetric Lorentz-rotation matrix. The matrix exponential is exponential damping times the corresponding rotation.

Boundary. Specify signed charge, field orientation, and rotation convention before component formulas.

Core result 2: Velocity covariance and thermal invariance

For stationary thermal velocity, the equal-time covariance matrix is (k_B*T/m) times identity. At positive lag the covariance is the drift matrix exponential times this equal-time covariance, so transverse correlations oscillate while decaying.

Boundary. Antisymmetric cross-correlations do not imply an antisymmetric covariance at equal times.

Core result 3: The symmetric diffusion tensor

With cyclotron frequency magnitude omega_c, longitudinal diffusion is k_B*T/(m*gamma) and transverse diffusion is (k_B*T/m)*gamma/(gamma squared+omega_c squared). The displacement-covariance diffusion tensor is the symmetric part of the integrated velocity-correlation matrix.

Boundary. An antisymmetric integrated response can encode Hall transport; it is not a contribution to mean-square displacement.

Laboratory. Use exact linear-Gaussian velocity updates to compare field-parallel and perpendicular displacement variances.

16 · Noise spectra, filters, and thermal fluctuationsReviewed

Read chapter 16 · Reviewed

Lectures 19 · Prerequisites: 6, 10, 12, 14

Infer a relaxation time from a noisy record without confusing spectral convention or finite-record artifacts with physics.

Wiener-Khinchin, white/colored noise, linear transfer functions, matrix spectra, resistor thermal noise.

Core result 1: Convention and Wiener-Khinchin

For a real stationary zero-mean process with integrable autocovariance C, define the two-sided angular-frequency spectral density S(omega) as the integral over all lag of C(tau)*exp(i*omega*tau). Then C(0) equals the integral of S divided by 2*pi under the corresponding inversion conditions.

Boundary. White noise is a generalized process; a nonzero mean adds a zero-frequency delta to the uncentered spectrum.

Core result 2: Linear filtering and matrix spectra

For a stable linear time-invariant filter with transfer matrix H and stationary input spectral matrix S_in, the output spectral matrix is H*S_in*H conjugate-transpose. A valid spectral matrix is Hermitian and positive semidefinite at each frequency where defined.

Boundary. Transfer formulas require a stationary output and appropriate stability/integrability assumptions.

Core result 3: Thermal-noise normalization

In the classical low-frequency equilibrium limit, the resistor voltage-noise density is 2*k_B*T*R in the two-sided angular-frequency convention used here; the corresponding one-sided density per hertz is 4*k_B*T*R. Integrating a physically filtered spectrum gives finite measured variance.

Boundary. The ideal white spectrum is bandwidth-limited physically; quantum and frequency-dependent corrections lie outside this approximation.

Laboratory. Compare covariance integration, a periodogram averaged over ensembles, and an analytic OU Lorentzian.

17 · Causality, susceptibility, and fluctuation-dissipationReviewed

Read chapter 17 · Reviewed

Lectures 20 · Prerequisites: 10, 11, 16

Predict the weak driven motion of a Brownian particle from its spontaneous fluctuations.

Dynamic mobility, causal response, generalized susceptibility, first/second fluctuation-dissipation relations, analyticity, classical response, LCR example.

Core result 1: A retarded response kernel

A causal linear response has change in mean observable at time t equal to the integral over nonnegative lag of response kernel times applied field at t minus lag. With the positive-exponential Fourier convention, susceptibility is the one-sided transform of this kernel.

Boundary. Linear response is a small-perturbation statement around a specified reference state.

Core result 2: Fluctuations and dissipation

For a classical equilibrium coordinate coupled to a force through energy -force times coordinate, its centered two-sided spectrum satisfies S_x(omega)=2*k_B*T*Im(chi_x(omega))/omega for nonzero real omega in the stated convention, with the zero-frequency limit taken when it exists.

Boundary. The observable, conjugate field, equilibrium hypothesis, and transform sign determine the formula; do not transplant it to arbitrary driven noise.

Core result 3: Causality constrains frequency response

For a sufficiently decaying real causal response kernel, the positive-exponential susceptibility is analytic in the upper complex-frequency half-plane and obeys conjugate symmetry on the real axis. Dispersion relations require the corresponding decay or subtraction hypotheses.

Boundary. A truncated numerical Hilbert transform is a check with bandwidth error, not a proof of a dispersion relation.

Laboratory. Compare equilibrium spectral estimates with a weak periodic-force response and identify nonlinear-drive failure.

18 · Random pulses, counting cumulants, and shot noiseReviewed

Read chapter 18 · Reviewed

Lectures 21 · Prerequisites: 1, 6, 16

Separate information about pulse shape from information about event timing in a detector's noise spectrum.

Poisson pulse sequences, Campbell cumulants, Carson spectrum, rectangular pulses, Barkhausen example, integrated dichotomous process bridge.

Core result 1: Poisson pulses and mean level

For stationary Poisson arrivals on the real time axis of intensity lambda, independent identically distributed amplitudes A, and an integrable pulse shape h, the signal sum A_j*h(t-T_j) has mean lambda*E[A] times the integral of h when the required integrability conditions hold.

Boundary. Starting arrivals at time zero produces a transient; it is not the stationary construction.

Core result 2: Covariance and cumulants

Under square-integrable h and finite amplitude second moment, the centered covariance is lambda*E[A squared] times the integral of h(s)*h(s+tau) ds. At one time, the kth cumulant is lambda*E[A raised to k] times the integral of h raised to k when finite.

Boundary. Use amplitude raw moments in these formulas, not amplitude cumulants; correlated arrivals change the result.

Core result 3: Pulse spectra and persistent motion

The centered shot-noise spectrum is lambda*E[A squared]*absolute(H(omega)) squared for pulse transform H. A nonzero mean contributes a separate delta at zero frequency to the uncentered spectrum. Integrating a two-state velocity process creates a continuous position process with memory in its velocity.

Boundary. A finite-record zero-frequency bin is not a literal Dirac delta; distinguish centered and uncentered estimates.

Laboratory. Simulate stationary shot noise with burn-in and boundary padding; compare mean, covariance, and spectral envelope.

19 · Persistent walks and finite-speed diffusionReviewed

Read chapter 19 · Reviewed

Lectures 22 · Prerequisites: 6, 10, 18

Construct transport with finite propagation speed and determine when ordinary diffusion becomes accurate.

Poisson covariance, persistent lattice walk, dichotomous diffusion equations, initial data, exact law, MSD, diffusion limit.

Core result 1: Velocity as the missing state variable

For velocity plus or minus v with symmetric switching rate nu, the two position densities satisfy coupled advection-switching equations. Their sum rho satisfies partial_tt rho+2*nu*partial_t rho=v squared*partial_xx rho, with two initial conditions inherited from the initial position and velocity law.

Boundary. The second-order equation alone does not specify initial velocity bias; position is not a Markov state by itself.

Core result 2: Atoms at the propagation fronts

Starting at the origin with equally likely initial velocities, the position law has atoms of mass exp(-nu*t)/2 at each of plus and minus v*t, in addition to a continuous interior component for positive time.

Boundary. A histogram or smooth density formula that omits the atoms is incomplete at finite time.

Core result 3: The ballistic-to-diffusive crossover

The displacement mean square for symmetric stationary initial velocity is (v squared/nu)*(t-(1-exp(-2*nu*t))/(2*nu)). At long time the effective diffusion coefficient is v squared/(2*nu); at short time the mean square is v squared*t squared.

Boundary. A Gaussian diffusion limit describes the central scaling region and does not preserve finite-speed fronts.

Laboratory. Compare event-driven telegraph trajectories with the density's continuous part and ballistic front masses.

20 · First passage, absorbing boundaries, and renewalReviewed

Read chapter 20 · Reviewed

Lectures 23, 24 · Prerequisites: 6, 9, 12, 19

Choose between eventual arrival probability, typical arrival time, and mean arrival time for the same target.

Telegraph crossings, first-passage density and moments, lattice backward equations, renewal transforms, random walk dimension and fractal introduction.

Core result 1: Arrival is a stopping event

For one-dimensional Brownian diffusion of coefficient D starting distance a positive from an absorbing target on an otherwise unbounded line, the first-arrival density is a*exp(-a squared/(4*D*t))/sqrt(4*pi*D*t cubed) for t positive. Its total probability is one while its mean time diverges.

Boundary. A finite simulation cutoff can produce a finite sample mean even when the mathematical mean is infinite.

Core result 2: The backward generator answers a different question

For Brownian diffusion in an interval (0,L) absorbed at both endpoints, the mean first-exit time solves D*T''=-1 with T(0)=T(L)=0 and equals x*(L-x)/(2*D). The probability of first exiting at L is x/L.

Boundary. Changing either boundary to reflecting changes the problem; forward and backward operators act on different arguments.

Core result 3: Renewal and Laplace transforms

For a time-homogeneous Markov process with a regenerative first-arrival decomposition at the target and appropriate transition density or mass, the target propagator is the time convolution of first-arrival law with the target-to-target propagator. Laplace transforms turn this convolution into a product.

Boundary. Point targets in higher-dimensional continuous space and non-Markov observations need separate hitting and renewal assumptions; fractal scaling is not Euclidean by default.

Laboratory. Compare exact absorbing-interval results with numerical backward equations; demonstrate bias from discrete-time missed crossings.

21 · Return laws, recurrence, and fractal transportReviewed

Read chapter 21 · Reviewed

Lectures 25 · Prerequisites: 3, 6, 20

Determine whether a walk returns almost surely even when its mean return time is infinite.

Sierpinski walk dimension, first-passage/return generating functions, recurrence criterion, biased/unbiased line walks and divergent means.

Core result 1: A first return excludes time zero

For a discrete-time Markov chain started at state i, let u_n be the probability of being at i at step n with u_0=1 and f_n its first-return probability for n at least one. The renewal identity is u_n equal to the sum over k=1 through n of f_k*u_(n-k); hence U(z)=1/(1-F(z)) for absolute z less than one.

Boundary. A return time starts after the initial visit; including time zero destroys the first-return definition.

Core result 2: Almost-sure return and infinite mean

The state is recurrent exactly when the sum of its return probabilities u_n diverges. For the unbiased nearest-neighbor walk on the integer line, return is almost sure but the mean positive return time is infinite. A biased walk with positive step probabilities p and 1-p has return probability 2*min(p,1-p).

Boundary. Recurrence does not imply a finite mean return time or a normalizable invariant law on an infinite lattice.

Core result 3: Geometry changes the walk scale

On a finite absorbing graph with transient transition submatrix K_TT in the book's column convention, expected exit times satisfy (identity-K_TT transpose)*T=ones. Scaling these solutions across graph sizes defines a numerical walk-dimension estimate rather than a theorem by itself.

Boundary. Separate graph distance, embedding distance, finite-size fit, and exact asymptotic walk dimension.

Laboratory. Compute first-return probabilities by absorbing-chain methods and explore transport scaling on finite gasket approximations.

22 · Recurrence by dimension and random walk clocksReviewed

Read chapter 22 · Reviewed

Lectures 26 · Prerequisites: 3, 6, 20, 21

Separate the geometry of a lattice walk from the clock that determines its physical-time progress.

Return on d-dimensional lattices, recurrence/transience threshold, continuous-time walks, exponential and nonexponential waiting times.

Core result 1: The return-probability criterion in dimension d

The symmetric nearest-neighbor walk on the integer d-dimensional lattice is recurrent for d=1 or d=2 and transient for d at least 3. The local return probability on its allowed even times decays proportionally to n raised to -d/2 at large n.

Boundary. A continuum Gaussian density at a point is not itself a lattice return probability; the allowed-time lattice normalization matters.

Core result 2: A random number of jumps

For independent identically distributed jumps and independent identically distributed positive waiting times, the position at time t is the sum of the first N(t) jumps, where N(t) is the renewal count. Exponential waiting times give a memoryless clock and a continuous-time Markov walk.

Boundary. Spatial jump recurrence and elapsed-time return moments are different questions.

Core result 3: Age restores information about the clock

For a waiting density psi with survival function Psi positive at age a, the conditional instantaneous event rate is psi(a)/Psi(a). This hazard is constant for an exponential law; for a general waiting law the position alone omits the elapsed waiting age needed to determine future jump timing.

Boundary. A non-Markov position process can often be embedded in a larger Markov state containing age, but existence and boundary details must be specified.

Laboratory. Estimate lattice return Green functions with parity handled correctly; attach exponential and heavy-tailed clocks to identical spatial jumps.

23 · Renewal memory and anomalous diffusionReviewed

Read chapter 23 · Reviewed

Lectures 27 · Prerequisites: 1, 3, 20, 22

Diagnose subdiffusion without interpreting a biased walk's mean-square displacement as its centered variance.

Biased continuous-time random walks, Fourier-Laplace solution, mean/variance asymptotics, non-Markovian and subdiffusive criteria.

Core result 1: The Fourier-Laplace renewal solution

For a decoupled continuous-time walk started at a renewal at the origin, jump characteristic function phi(k), and waiting-time Laplace transform psi_tilde(s), its position transform is (1-psi_tilde(s))/(s*(1-psi_tilde(s)*phi(k))) for positive s, under the usual nonexplosive renewal construction.

Boundary. Aged initial conditions and coupled jump/wait laws change this formula.

Core result 2: Mean and variance from a random sum

For independent jumps with finite mean mu_J and variance sigma_J squared, independent of the renewal clock, mean position is mu_J*E[N(t)] and centered position variance is sigma_J squared*E[N(t)]+mu_J squared*Var(N(t)).

Boundary. Uncentered mean-square displacement also contains the squared mean; bias changes scaling conclusions.

Core result 3: An infinite-mean waiting scale

If the waiting transform obeys 1-psi_tilde(s) asymptotic to (tau0*s)^alpha for alpha in (0,1), standard renewal Tauberian hypotheses yield E[N(t)] asymptotic to (t/tau0)^alpha/Gamma(1+alpha). For zero-mean finite-variance independent jumps this gives centered displacement variance asymptotic to sigma_J squared times that expression.

Boundary. A power-law fit over a short window does not establish an asymptotic exponent or ergodicity; biased clocks add a separate renewal-count variance term.

Laboratory. Compare ensemble displacement, centered variance, and time averages under exponential and infinite-mean clocks.

24 · Deterministic maps, invariant measures, and coarse observationsReviewed

Read chapter 24 · Reviewed

Lectures 28 · Prerequisites: 2, 5, 21

Explain how deterministic evolution can produce probabilistic observations without replacing the dynamics by an arbitrary random model.

Flows and maps, coarse graining, invariant measure, Frobenius-Perron evolution, ergodicity, escape/return definitions, Poincare statement.

Core result 1: Pushforward probability

A measurable map T transports a probability measure mu to a measure assigning each measurable set A the value mu(T inverse of A). An invariant measure satisfies mu(T inverse of A)=mu(A) for every measurable A.

Boundary. An invariant density depends on the reference measure and need not exist for every invariant measure.

Core result 2: The transfer operator counts preimages

For a piecewise smooth map with regular inverse branches and nonzero branch derivatives, density transfer at y is the sum of input density at each inverse point divided by the absolute map derivative. For the doubling map it is one half of rho(y/2)+rho((y+1)/2).

Boundary. Coarse-grained cells need not form an exact Markov chain; finite-precision periodicity is a numerical artifact to examine explicitly.

Core result 3: Ergodicity and recurrence are separate

For a measure-preserving map on a probability space, ergodicity means every invariant measurable set has measure zero or one. Under the ergodic theorem, time averages of integrable observables equal their invariant-measure mean almost everywhere when the map is ergodic.

Boundary. Measure preservation alone yields recurrence in finite measure but does not imply ergodicity or mixing.

Laboratory. Evolve densities under the doubling map and compare exact symbolic cylinders with finite-precision orbit experiments.

25 · Sojourn, escape, and recurrence in deterministic dynamicsReviewed

Read chapter 25 · Reviewed

Lectures 29 · Prerequisites: 20, 21, 24

Choose the correct entrance-conditioned statistic when measuring residence and return times from a coarse-grained orbit.

Sojourn and escape distributions, recurrence differences, Poincare proof, Smoluchowski modification, Bernoulli shift example.

Core result 1: Sampling residence intervals

For an independent binary occupancy sequence with occupied probability q in (0,1), the occupied-run length observed at an entrance has probability (1-q)*q raised to (n-1) for positive n. An interval sampled at an arbitrary occupied time is length-biased relative to the entrance-sampled interval.

Boundary. Define discrete endpoints before differencing sojourn probabilities; memoryless residual lengths do not eliminate length bias of total intervals.

Core result 2: Finite-measure recurrence

For a measure-preserving transformation on a finite-measure space and a measurable set A, almost every point of A returns to A infinitely often. This requires neither independent visits nor ergodicity.

Boundary. The theorem does not bound return time and fails without its finite invariant measure hypothesis.

Core result 3: Mean returns and correlated observations

For an ergodic measure-preserving transformation on a probability space and A of positive measure, the conditional mean positive return time to A is 1/mu(A), under the standard Kac return-time theorem. Independent Bernoulli visits provide a geometric example; overlapping symbolic cylinders generally produce correlated visits.

Boundary. Kac's formula needs its precise measure-theoretic conditions; any Smoluchowski or finite-difference sojourn formula must be verified against the lecture's endpoint and conditioning conventions before drafting.

Laboratory. Enumerate symbolic cylinders and compare residence, first-return, and arbitrary-time residual residence laws.
